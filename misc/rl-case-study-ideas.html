Cashing the overhang

Where an RL “clever trick” can still live in the Sanskrit project — and the three experiments that could demonstrate it.

The situation in one picture

The policy already contains most of the remaining capability: given 16 tries and the vidyut verifier, it solves 92% of tasks. It just doesn’t rank the right answer first. That 31-point gap is the sampling overhang — and converting overhang into pass@1 is exactly the job RL is supposed to do (“distribution sharpening”). Our GRPO didn’t do it.

Why it didn’t move: advantage collapse

GRPO learns from the spread of rewards inside each 8-rollout group. On a strong policy most groups come back uniform — and a uniform group has zero advantage, hence zero gradient. This isn’t a hypothesis: we logged every group of both round-2 runs (reward_history.jsonl, 1,080 steps each). 32.7% of vp_exact steps and 26.3% of vp_chrf steps had exactly zero reward spread — and of those dead groups, 81% (exact) and 97% (chrf) were saturated successes: all 8 rollouts already perfect, nothing to learn. The literature has a name for this (advantage collapse, gradient starvation) and a fix (DAPO’s dynamic sampling).

Three candidate tricks

A · Frontier-filtered GRPO — train only where the gradient exists

~$35 one A100win probability: highestlit: DAPO dynamic sampling, made cheap & offline

Use the verifier to pre-score every training task under the current policy (16 samples each), then run GRPO only on the frontier band — tasks solved 1–15 times out of 16. Every group is guaranteed non-uniform, so every step carries signal. Bundle the known config fixes (lr 5e-5, KL anneal, groups ≥16) into the same arm.

Case-study line: “GRPO stalled at +0.0 on a strong SFT policy; we show the gradient had collapsed, then revive it with one verifier-driven curriculum trick and convert X points of overhang into pass@1.”

B · Rejection-sampling FT vs GRPO — is policy gradient even the right amortizer?

~$30 (shares A’s sampling pass)win probability: goodlit: STaR / RFT / RAFT-vs-GRPO debate

Sample 16 completions per training task, keep only vidyut-verified correct ones, and SFT on them. No policy gradients, no KL, no advantage — the verifier does all the selecting. Head-to-head against GRPO at matched compute, it directly asks whether the failure was policy-gradient mechanics or something deeper.

Case-study line: “At 4B with a strong SFT init, verifier-filtered self-distillation cashes the overhang that policy-gradient RL could not” — or, if GRPO-with-A wins instead, the controlled comparison is the story.

C · Free process rewards from the Paninian engine — the original one

~$40 + dev timewin probability: medium (feasibility check first)lit: process reward models — but ours needs no training

Math/code RLVR uses outcome-only rewards because checking intermediate steps is expensive. This domain is special: vidyut-prakriya emits the entire derivation sequence (the prakriyā). Score completions by how deep their derivation matches the engine’s — dense, exact, training-free process supervision. All-wrong groups stop being zero-signal: a completion that diverges at step 6 outranks one that diverges at step 2.

Case-study line: “Symbolic engines — grammars, compilers, proof assistants — are free process reward models.” The most generalizable claim on this page, and unique to a domain with an exact derivation engine.
Recommendation: run A + B together on one A100 (~$60–70 total). The 16-sample sweep A needs for difficulty scores is literally the dataset B trains on, so the second experiment is nearly free. Three-way contrast (frontier-GRPO vs RFT vs the existing null) guarantees a story no matter which arm wins. C is the high-originality follow-up once an optimization loop demonstrably moves.

The framing that makes even the null result a chapter

pass@16 with the verifier is a deployable system today: best-of-16 + vidyut check = 92%, at 16× inference cost. So the whole project frames as: RL is the amortization of verifier search into the policy. Search works (92%). Amortization-by-GRPO failed for a diagnosable reason (advantage collapse). The trick — A, B, or C — is the amortizer that works. That framing turns the existing negative result into Act 2 rather than a dead end.

What the campaign can already claim (no new compute)

  • Independent replication of four 2025–26 RLVR findings in a new domain (Paninian derivation, 4B, low-resource): pass@k inversion (Yue et al., pass@k inversion), advantage collapse (diagnosis), reward-design insensitivity (Spurious Rewards), and replay/mixture SFT dominating narrow SFT + RL (forgetting mitigation, Quagmires for the val-loss trap).
  • A novel evaluation-integrity failure mode: the trainer mutates its base-model directory on disk, so six independent-looking evaluations shared one poisoned input and agreed on a wrong number. “The most dangerous eval bug is the one that replicates.”
