Your goal, in your words: present something cool — Lean proving a real guarantee — starting from something realistic, ideally a claim where formalization actually settles a dispute. 2.A 3-minute primer (no background assumed)

The Aṣṭādhyāyī. A grammar of Sanskrit written by Pāṇini around the 4th century BCE. It is ~4,000 terse rules (sūtras) that, like a little program, generate correct Sanskrit words from roots and endings. Scholars treat it as the first formal, generative description of any language.
Object-language vs. metalanguage. The thing being described is Sanskrit; the language Pāṇini describes it in is a tightly-defined technical shorthand — its own symbols and conventions. Bhate & Kak put it plainly: “its end language (the object language) and the means language (the metalanguage) are distinctly different from each other.” This clean separation is exactly what makes formalization feasible — there's a precise symbol system to model.
Sandhi. When sounds meet at boundaries they fuse by fixed phonetic rules (English “a apple” → “an apple” is a faint cousin). E.g. tava + indra → tavendra. These rules are the cleanest “rewriting system” inside the grammar.
Kāraka. The who-did-what-to-whom roles (agent, object, instrument…) marked by case-endings — covered in Part II; not the focus here.

The one piece you'll need that's not obvious — because the star claim is about it — is the Śivasūtras. The next section teaches it from scratch.

3.The star claim, explained: the Śivasūtras

Pāṇini's rules constantly need to say things like “any vowel” or “any consonant” or “any stop.” Writing out the sound-list every time would be enormous. So before the grammar proper, he lists all the sounds of Sanskrit in 14 short lines, each ending in a single marker letter (an anubandha, shown here in maroon). These 14 lines are the Śivasūtras.

The trick: a pratyāhāra is an abbreviation built from [a sound] + [some later marker]; it names the entire contiguous run of sounds from that sound up to that marker. Examples:

  • aC = from a (line 1) up to marker C (end of line 4) = a i u ṛ ḷ e o ai au — exactly the 9 vowels. So Pāṇini writes “aC” to mean “any vowel.”
  • haL = from h (line 5) to the final marker L = exactly the consonants.
  • aL = from a to L = every sound.
What a “natural class” is. A natural class is a set of sounds that the grammar's rules keep treating as a single unit — the vowels, the consonants, the stops, the nasals, the voiced sounds, and so on. The test is behavioral, not a priori: a group counts as “natural” when many rules quantify over exactly that group (Pāṇini's say things like “after any vowel…”, “before any voiced consonant…”), so giving it a name pays for itself many times over. A group you could only define by listing its members — “k, ḷ, and au” — is not natural; no rule ever wants it. Formally each class is just a subset of the ~50 sounds, and Pāṇini's rules lean on about 42 of them. A pratyāhāra is his two-letter device for naming such a class — but it can only name a class whose members happen to sit in a contiguous stretch of the alphabet. That constraint is the whole game.

This only works if the sounds are ordered so that every group Pāṇini ever needs (one of those ~42 natural classes) happens to form a contiguous interval. That is a strong constraint — and the claim is that Pāṇini's particular ordering does it optimally, needing only a single sound (h) to be repeated. Section 4 scores that claim; here's why it's the one to chase: it's pure combinatorics over a fixed list of sounds — no need to model the 4,000 derivational rules at all.

②  The Śivasūtra ordering is optimal  recommended target

Context. (See §3.) Among all the ways to order Sanskrit's sounds so that the needed groups are contiguous intervals, Pāṇini's needs the fewest “tricks” — only one sound (h) repeated.

The claim. Wiebke Petersen proved formally (pen-and-paper, 2004) that Pāṇini's ordering is an optimal S-alphabet — fewest duplications + fewest markers for the natural classes he uses. It is genuinely controversial in the good way: Staal, Kiparsky, and Petersen agree the order is systematic but disagree on the criterion that makes it optimal. ③  The whole grammar is maximally economical (“shortest grammar”)  avoid (as stated)

Context. Pāṇini is legendary for brevity (lāghava). The tradition is almost romantic about it:

“Grammarians celebrate the saving of half a mora like the birth of a son.” maxim ardhamātrālāghavena putrotsavaṃ manyante vaiyākaraṇāḥ, Paribhāṣenduśekhara — wisdomlib
“the fundamental principle of economy (simplicity, lāghava), which governs Pāṇini's entire grammatical system” … “the economy principle, by selecting the shortest grammar, determines both the ordering of sounds and the placement of markers.” Kiparsky, siva-t.pdf

Why to avoid it as a target. As a global theorem it isn't a precise proposition — “shortest possible grammar of Sanskrit” has no fixed formal statement, and Kiparsky himself documents places where economy over-applies (“vacuous overgeneralization”), so a clean “no rule is redundant” is false. The economy that is provable is the local, bounded kind — and the cleanest instance of it is exactly target ② (the Śivasūtras). The “factor-of-3 compression” from anuvṛtti (rule-gapping) is real but illustrative, not a theorem.

Proved? No (global) — only the Śivasūtra sub-case is proven.
Mechanized? No
Controversial? Yes — Cardona vs Kiparsky on economy-vs-generalization; Kiparsky even recants his 1979 view.
difficulty5no precise global statement to even prove
controversy3real, but about interpretation
impact2ill-posed; collapses into ②

④  The grammar is deterministic (one correct form per meaning)  good 2nd step (fragment)

Context. Given a fully specified intended meaning, the grammar should produce a single correct word — no ambiguity. (Part II showed this is achieved by ordering/conflict-resolution, not confluence.)

“[the grammar] generates a single derivation for every grammatical sentence of Sanskrit” … but “the rules are non-deterministic, having several possibilities.” Shyamasundar, Computing Processes and Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī — Bhāvanā (note the self-qualification)

As a target. Over the full grammar this is unproven and huge. Over a small sandhi fragment (a handful of vowel-coalescence rules + the “later-rule-wins” tie-break) it's a clean, provable “unique normal form” theorem — and a perfect bridge from ② to the harder stuff. Scope it tiny.

Proved? No — asserted, explicitly qualified; never proven even informally for the whole system.

⑧  Rajpopat's conflict-resolution algorithm “solves” the grammar  north star — max controversy

Context. When two rules both apply, which wins? In 2022 a Cambridge PhD, Rishi Rajpopat, claimed to crack this with a new reading of sūtra 1.4.2 — and the press ran with “2,500-year-old problem solved.”

“If two rules are simultaneously applicable at a given step in a Pāṇinian derivation, which of the two should be applied?” … his rule: “in case of DOI (vipratiṣedha), the right-hand side (para) operation prevails.” Rajpopat, In Pāṇini We Trust (thesis abstract) — Cambridge repository
“Pāṇini's language machine produced grammatically correct words with almost no exceptions.” University of Cambridge press release — cam.ac.uk (“almost,” not “all”)

The controversy is real and named. Senior Sanskritists pushed back hard — both on the “solved” framing and the technical substance:

“to account for Rishi's new interpretation, certain inconvenient rules in the Aṣṭādhyāyī are then labeled as possible interpolations.” Madhav Deshpande (Univ. of Michigan), INDOLOGY/bvparishat listserv — thread
the algorithm “needs exhaustive testing with 1,00,000 to 5,00,000 more examples before it can be termed sound and working.” Neelesh Bodas, reported in Samvada / HinduPost

Why it's a tantalizing — but unstable — formalization target. His claim is, in principle, exactly mechanizable: encode both the traditional “later-rule-wins” and his “right-operand-wins” functions, run them over a corpus of derivations, and measure the exception rate. Nobody has done this. But the ground is unstable: the claim's generality depends on relabeling contradicting rules as “interpolations,” so the rule set itself is contested — and you'd need a large derivation engine first. This is a dream Year-2 result, not a Year-1 one.

Proved? No — empirical “almost no exceptions,” unpublished, not peer-reviewed.
Mechanized? No — and nobody has measured it at scale.
Controversial? Maximally — named scholars (Scharf, Deshpande, Lowe) on record disputing it.
difficulty5needs a derivation engine + a contested rule set
controversy5the hottest claim in the field
impact5a measured verdict would make news

6.Target ② in depth — the claim, the live dispute, and the proof

This is the single home for everything about the recommended target. The catalogue card (§4) gives the one-line verdict and the score; here is the whole thing — what is actually claimed, exactly who disagrees with whom and over what, and how the Lean development is built so that “optimal” stops being a slogan and becomes a statement you could machine-check. The reassuring news: the whole problem lives over a fixed, finite list of sounds — no derivational rule is modelled. The subtle news: the optimality half of the theorem quantifies over infinitely many rival alphabets, and handling that cleanly is the one real piece of proof craft. We build up to it.

6.1  The competing claims: what does “optimal” even mean?

Everyone who has studied the Śivasūtras agrees on the surprising fact — the ordering is systematic, not an accident — and on one concrete consequence: the sound h is the only one that must appear twice. What the three leading scholars dispute is the criterion: in what sense, and for which reasons, the ordering counts as optimal. Stated plainly, side by side:

The primary sources, in their own words. First, what Petersen actually proved:

“We present a formal argument which shows that, using his representation method, Pāṇini's way of ordering the phonological segments to represent the natural classes is optimal.”
… “PROPOSITION 4.2. Pāṇini's Śivasūtras form an optimal S-alphabet.” Petersen, A Mathematical Analysis of Pāṇini's Śivasūtras, J. Logic Lang. Inf. 2004 — open PDF, abstract & §4.2

Her “optimal” pointedly does not mean “shortest” — the distinction that the whole dispute turns on:

“optimal S-alphabets do not necessarily minimize the overall length of the S-alphabet.” Petersen 2004, Definition 2.5 — optimality = fewest duplicated sounds + fewest markers, relative to the 42 classes Pāṇini actually used

Kiparsky agrees with Staal that the order is systematic but, against Staal, insists no extra principle beyond economy is needed:

“In this paper I shall argue, like Staal, that the structure of the Śivasūtras is entirely explicable on systematic grounds. However, I shall try to show that no other principles are needed than … the principle of economy and the logic of the special case and the general case.” Kiparsky, Economy and the Construction of the Śivasūtras — PDF (disputes Staal's extra phonetic criterion and Cardona's historical one)

And on the one thing they do agree on — that the repetition of h is forced — Petersen's proof and Kiparsky's argument converge from different directions:

“This is the only repetition which is necessary in the system.” Kiparsky, op. cit.  /  “there is no other choice than duplicating h to get an optimal S-alphabet corresponding to Pāṇini's pratyāhāras.” Petersen 2004, §4.2
Why this dispute is the reason to mechanize, not a reason to avoid. Mechanizing forces you to write down exactly which set of natural classes (paniniClasses) and which optimality criterion (cost) you assume — the two things the three scholars argue about in prose. A Lean development turns “optimal in what sense?” from a debate into a parameter: “under Petersen's criterion + this class set → optimal (proved); swap in a length-minimizing cost → the theorem is false; assume Staal's extra principle → you additionally need X.” That is formalization doing what it is best at — making a vague disagreement checkable. §6.4 shows the criterion entering the statement as a literal hypothesis.

6.2  The objects you model

Four definitions carry the entire development. A sound is a constructor of a finite inductive type; a class is just a set of sounds; an S-alphabet is a linear arrangement of sounds with markers sitting between them; and a pratyāhāra reads off the contiguous block from a starting sound up to a marker.

-- The fixed varṇa inventory the Śivasūtras arrange (≈50 sounds).
inductive Sound
  | a | i | u | rv | lv | e | o | ai | au          -- the 9 vowels
  | h | y | v | r | l | velarN | m | retroN | n     -- semivowels + nasals
  | jh | bh | gh | retroDh | dh | j | b | g | retroD | d
  | kh | ph | ch | retroTh | th | c | retroT | t    -- the stops
  | palatalS | retroS | s                          -- the sibilants
deriving DecidableEq, Fintype

-- A natural class is nothing more than a finite set of sounds.
abbrev Class := Finset Sound

-- The 14 anubandha markers (Ṇ, K, Ṅ, C, Ṭ, … L).
inductive Marker | N1 | K | Ng | C | T1 | N2 | M | Ny | Sh | Sx | V | Y | R | L
deriving DecidableEq, Fintype

-- An S-alphabet: a list whose entries are either a sound or a marker.
-- (`shivasutras : SAlphabet` is then literally the 14 lines, flattened.)
inductive Item | snd (s : Sound) | mark (m : Marker)
abbrev SAlphabet := List Item

The pratyāhāra denoted by “sound s + marker m” is every sound from the first occurrence of s up to (but not including) the first m after it:

def pratyahara (A : SAlphabet) (s : Sound) (m : Marker) : Class :=
  -- drop everything before the first `snd s`; from the rest, collect the
  -- sounds appearing strictly before the first `mark m`.
  ((A.dropWhile (· ≠ .snd s)).takeWhile (· ≠ .mark m)).filterMap Item.toSound
    |>.toFinset

6.3  The stepping stone, in two lines

Because everything is finite and DecidableEq, target ① (“the abbreviations denote what the textbooks say”) is not a proof you write — it's a computation Lean runs. This is the satisfying first commit, and it doubles as the README demo:

-- aC = the nine vowels.  haL = the consonants.  Both checked by `decide`.
example : pratyahara shivasutras .a .C
        = {.a, .i, .u, .rv, .lv, .e, .o, .ai, .au} := by decide

example : pratyahara shivasutras .h .L
        = (Finset.univ.filter (¬ ·.isVowel)) := by decide

The kernel evaluates the interval and compares finite sets — no human reasoning in the loop. That is exactly why ① scores difficulty 1: it's a decidable check over fixed data.

6.4  What “optimal” means, written down

Now the headline. First say what it is to solve the problem at all, then what it is to solve it best. An alphabet encodes a class if some pratyāhāra equals it; it is an S-alphabet for a family 𝒞 if it encodes every class in 𝒞 and lists every sound at least once:

def Encodes (A : SAlphabet) (C : Class) : Prop :=
  ∃ s m, pratyahara A s m = C

def IsSAlphabet (A : SAlphabet) (𝒞 : Finset Class) : Prop :=
  (∀ C ∈ 𝒞, Encodes A C) ∧ (∀ s : Sound, .snd s ∈ A)

The cost Petersen minimizes is not length (Def. 2.5 in the paper says so explicitly). It is the number of markers used plus the number of sounds that had to be repeated — a duplication being the expensive “trick” the whole tradition wants to avoid:

def cost (A : SAlphabet) : Nat :=
  A.markerCount + A.duplications        -- duplications = Σ_s max(0, count s − 1)

-- Petersen, Proposition 4.2, as one Lean statement.
theorem shivasutra_optimal :
    IsSAlphabet shivasutras paniniClasses ∧
    (∀ A, IsSAlphabet A paniniClasses → cost shivasutras ≤ cost A) := ...
Read the theorem like a contract. Everything contestable is now a named hypothesis — paniniClasses (which 42 classes you assume Pāṇini needed) and the definition of cost (which notion of optimal). The Staal / Kiparsky / Petersen disagreement isn't argued away; it's relocated into the two definitions you must pin down before the theorem typechecks. Swap cost for a length-minimizing version and the theorem becomes false — which is itself the cleanest possible statement of what's at stake.

6.5  The proof, in three moves

Why is it true? The argument is combinatorial, and it factors into three intuitions you can hold in your head.

Move 1 — encoding a class = making it an interval. A pratyāhāra is, by construction, a contiguous block. So “A encodes C” means precisely “the members of C occupy a run of consecutive positions in A ending at a marker.” The entire problem is therefore the classical question: given a family of sets, find a linear order of the ground elements making every set an interval. That is the consecutive-ones property, and it is exactly where the difficulty — and the beautiful structure — lives.

Move 2 — crossings are what force duplication. Say two classes cross when they overlap but neither contains the other (e.g. {x,y} and {y,z}). A family with no crossings is laminar (every pair is nested or disjoint), and a laminar family can always be laid out with every set an interval and zero duplications — you just nest the intervals like matching brackets. So duplications are needed only to break crossings. The content of Petersen's result is that Pāṇini's 42 classes are laminar except for essentially one unavoidable crossing, and the unique element you can repeat to sever it is h — which is why h, and only h, appears twice (lines 5 and 14).

-- The duplication lemma: every S-alphabet for Pāṇini's classes repeats h.
theorem h_must_repeat (A : SAlphabet)
    (hA : IsSAlphabet A paniniClasses) :
    2 ≤ (A.filter (· = .snd .h)).length := by
  -- Suppose h occurred once. The class of sounds entering the y/v/r rules and
  -- the class entering the aspiration rules both must be intervals; with a single
  -- h they cross, so no linear order makes both contiguous — contradiction.
  ...

Move 3 — markers are right-endpoints, and nesting lets them be shared. Each class, as an interval, needs a marker at its right edge. But two nested classes that share a right edge can share one marker. Minimizing markers is thus minimizing the number of distinct right-endpoints across the family — and the laminar (bracket-nesting) layout is what makes maximal sharing possible. Pāṇini's 14 markers are the floor for his class family; Move 2 supplies the one forced duplication; together they are cost shivasutras, and no rival can go lower. That conjunction is shivasutra_optimal.

6.6  The one hazard worth naming up front

The lower bound ranges over infinitely many alphabets. ∀ A, IsSAlphabet A … → cost shivasutras ≤ cost A quantifies over all lists, of which there are infinitely many, so you cannot close it with decide the way you closed §6.3. Two honest routes:
  • (a) Bound, then decide. Prove a normal-form lemma — “an optimal S-alphabet never repeats a sound it didn't have to, and never carries an unused marker” — which caps the length of any cost-optimal candidate. Finitely many candidates remain ⇒ a (large but) decidable search. Lower engineering risk, less insight.
  • (b) Port Petersen's structural argument. Formalize Moves 1–3 directly: define crossing/laminar families, prove the laminar-layout and shared-endpoint lemmas, derive the bound abstractly. More work, but it's the proof that explains the result rather than merely confirming it — and it's the version a linguistics audience would actually want.
Recommendation: do (a) first as a safety net that the number is right, then upgrade to (b) for the paper. The Mathlib pieces are all present — Finset, List, Fintype and Decidable instances for route (a); Order/lattice and SimpleGraph material for the crossing/laminar structure in route (b).
Concretely, what “done” looks like for target ②. A small Lean 4 repo that: (i) defines the 14 Śivasūtras and the pratyāhāra-interval function (§6.2); (ii) proves a handful of pratyāhāras denote their textbook sets — the stepping stone, and a great README demo (§6.3); (iii) defines “the natural classes Pāṇini uses” and an optimality predicate (fewest duplications + fewest markers, §6.4); (iv) proves Pāṇini's ordering satisfies it, with the h-duplication shown necessary (§6.5), stating up front — in the theorem's hypotheses — exactly which class-set and criterion you assume. That last clause is the scholarly payload: it makes the Staal / Kiparsky / Petersen disagreement of §6.1 visible as Lean hypotheses. A genuinely novel, defensible, blog-and-paper-worthy artifact — and a port, not a gamble.

The lovely thing: the easy rungs and the controversial target are the same object. You model the Śivasūtras once; proving the abbreviations work is the stepping stone; proving the ordering optimal is the headline. Nothing is wasted.

Honesty notes carried from the research. (1) Every “verbatim” quote above was pulled from a page an agent actually fetched; two originals (Petersen's Springer page, the BBC article) were only reachable via an open mirror / reproduction and are cited as such. (2) The Rajpopat critique quotes from Samvada/HinduPost are advocacy outlets; the Deshpande/Lowe quotes from the INDOLOGY listserv are the most reliable. (3) Petersen's optimality is real but conditional — on the 42 classes Pāṇini used and on her specific criterion; that conditionality is exactly what makes mechanizing it interesting, not a weakness to hide. (4) Still no theorem-prover formalization of any part of Pāṇini exists — so target ② would be a genuine first.
