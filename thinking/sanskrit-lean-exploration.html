1.The question that decides everything

Your mental model — “model C in Lean, then prove properties of C programs” — is the right instinct, but the load-bearing word is prove, not model. A Python interpreter can already run a toy language. What Lean (like Coq or Isabelle) uniquely gives you is the ability to state a property quantified over all programs / all derivations and have a machine check the proof. So before any Sanskrit idea, ask the filter question:

Does this task need me to prove a universal statement about a rule system, or just to run the rules on an input? If it's the latter, Lean is a heavy, exotic way to do what OCaml does better.

Hold onto that, because the research below shows that almost every concrete Sanskrit task you'd name — splitting words, parsing, identifying meter — already works fine in the left-hand box. The opportunity is exclusively in the right one.

2.Sanskrit really is a formal system

Your excitement is well-founded, and not in a hand-wavy way. Around the 4th century BCE, Pāṇini wrote the Aṣṭādhyāyī (“eight chapters”): roughly 4,000 sūtras that generate the well-formed words of Sanskrit from roots and affixes. It is, by wide scholarly agreement, a formal generative grammar — with features modern computer scientists would recognize instantly:

  • sūtras as rewrite rules — context-sensitive string replacement.
  • paribhāṣā — meta-rules that govern how the object-rules apply (rules about rules).
  • utsarga / apavāda — a general rule and its exception, with the specific blocking the general (Pāṇini's version of “most-specific-match wins”).
  • the tripādī and asiddhatva (sūtra 8.2.1, pūrvatrāsiddham): the last three quarters of the grammar are treated as “not yet having applied” relative to each other — an explicit, ordered, suspend-and-resume rule-ordering discipline, exactly like classical generative phonology.

This is not romanticism. Gerald Penn and Paul Kiparsky proved a careful result (COLING 2012) that Pāṇini's rule formalism, viewed as a “contextualized replacement system,” is strictly more powerful than context-free grammars and lands in the mildly-context-sensitive region. Kiparsky's “On the Architecture of Pāṇini's Grammar” is the standard structural analysis. Cardona and Staal began the formalization in the 1960s; Scharf & Hyman even encoded individual sūtras as a finite-state transducer with an XML rule vocabulary.

The myth you'll hit immediately — defuse it before someone else weaponizes it. You will be told “NASA proved Sanskrit is the best language for computers/AI.” This is false. The real source is Rick Briggs's 1985 AI Magazine paper, “Knowledge Representation in Sanskrit and Artificial Intelligence.” Its actual, defensible claim is narrow: the Sanskrit grammarians' kāraka / śābdabodha analysis of sentence meaning resembles AI semantic-network knowledge representation. Briggs never says Sanskrit is “the best computer language,” never speaks for NASA, and the viral quotes are fabricated — India's own education ministry has had to walk the claim back. Cite Briggs for what he wrote; never lean on the myth. It will discredit a good project.

3.Reality check: what's already solved without a prover

This is the most important section for keeping you honest. The field of Sanskrit Computational Linguistics is mature, with a long-running ISCLS symposium series (Springer LNAI). Three paradigms already cover the obvious tasks — and none of them uses a theorem prover:

TaskAlready solved byHowNeeds Lean?
Sandhi splitting / segmentation (undoing euphonic merging at word boundaries) Gérard Huet's Sanskrit Heritage reader; the neural TransLIST (SOTA) Finite-state transducers (“Eilenberg machines”); hybrid lexicon + Transformer No
Morphology (declension, conjugation, derivation) Heritage generators; Saṃsādhanī (A. Kulkarni) Finite-state + Pāṇinian rule engines; an Aṣṭādhyāyī simulator exists No
Kāraka / dependency parsing (who-did-what-to-whom) Kulkarni's dependency parser for verses Constraint solving over Pāṇinian rules No
Meter identification Melnad–Goyal–Scharf, Skrutable, Chandojñānam Linear scan of light/heavy syllables No
The negative result that defines your opening. My searches found no formalization of Pāṇini's grammar in Lean, Coq, Isabelle, or any proof assistant. The richly ironic part: Gérard Huet, who built the leading Sanskrit engine, is also a founding figure of the Calculus of Constructions behind Coq and Lean — yet he never pointed a proof assistant at Pāṇini. That gap is your real estate. Your pitch is not “I'll build a Sanskrit parser” (done, many times). It's “I'll prove the grammar's rule system has the properties everyone assumes it has.”

4.Direction A — MiniSanskrit & proving properties Strongest — once reframed

Your instinct is right; the framing needs one correction. A natural-language sentence is not a program — it doesn't “crash,” “return the wrong value,” or “leak memory.” So “prove properties of a piece of Sanskrit” doesn't transfer literally. What does transfer — and beautifully — is this: Pāṇini's grammar is a string-rewriting system, and rewriting systems are exactly the objects Lean loves to prove theorems about.

Don't try to model all 4,000 sūtras. Carve out a clean fragment — sandhi (the phonological rules that merge sounds at boundaries) is ideal. Model it as a small set of rewrite rules in Lean, then prove the meta-theorems that the tradition simply asserts:

Meta-theoremPlain-English meaningWhy it's a real Lean target
Termination (strong normalization)Applying sandhi rules always halts; you never loop forever.Needs a well-founded measure + induction — Lean's home turf.
Confluence (Church–Rosser)The final form doesn't depend on the order you apply rules.The diamond property; genuinely hard to verify by testing.
Uniqueness of normal formEvery input has exactly one fully-combined output.Follows from the two above — a clean, quotable result.
Faithfulness of asiddhatvaPāṇini's rule-ordering really does give the same result as the ordered phonology it models.An equivalence-of-two-semantics proof — the PL classic.

The prior art is directly transferable: people have formalized general & context-free grammars in Lean 4, and confluence/termination of rewriting is a deep, mature line (Coq's CoLoR; recent Lean 4 confluence frameworks). You'd be the first to point that machinery at a natural-language grammar with 2,400 years of pedigree. This is the flagship.

5.Direction B — a reward oracle for fine-tuning Real, but Lean is often not load-bearing

You're pointing at a hot and correct paradigm: Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). Axiom's AXLE (released March 2026) and its 12/12 Putnam-2025 result work because Lean is a sound oracle: a proof either type-checks or it doesn't, so the reward signal can't be gamed the way a learned reward model can.

Now the honest part. To fine-tune a model on Sanskrit you'd want a checker for things like “is this valid sandhi?”, “is this verse in Vasantatilakā meter?”, “do the case-endings agree?” Every one of those is decidable and can be written in plain Python. A decidable checker does not need a theorem prover. So the naïve version of B is a great project that doesn't use Lean — which violates your own constraint.

The version of B where Lean is load-bearing: proof-carrying Sanskrit. Instead of the model outputting a sentence and a Python script scoring it, the model must output a sentence together with a Pāṇinian derivation — a sequence of sūtra applications — that Lean mechanically checks. Reward = “the derivation type-checks in your verified grammar from Direction A.” Now Lean is essential, because:
  • the reward is a machine-checked proof of grammaticality, not a heuristic score;
  • the checker itself is proven correct (you can't reward-hack a bug in the grader);
  • you get a verified data flywheel — train on derivation-checked data without model collapse, exactly the AXLE thesis (RLVR survey).
This only exists if Direction A exists first. B is the payoff of A, not a standalone project.

6.Direction C — proving two sentences are “the same” Interesting if you pick the right notion of “same”

“Are these two sentences the same?” is, in full generality, not well-defined and not decidable — semantic/paraphrase equivalence of natural language is a swamp. If you mean it loosely, no formal system (Lean or otherwise) can deliver it, and you'll thrash.

But Sanskrit hands you a crisp, decidable notion of equivalence that other languages don't, thanks to one feature: free word order. Because each word carries its grammatical role in its case ending (vibhakti) rather than in its position, you can shuffle the words and the meaning is preserved. So define:

Two surface strings are equivalent iff (1) they segment (modulo sandhi) to the same multiset of inflected words, and (2) they induce the same kāraka / dependency structure — the same who-did-what-to-whom.

Kulkarni's group already computes kāraka structures (parser), so again — the parsing isn't the Lean part. The Lean part is: a verified definition of equivalence, a proof it's a genuine equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric, transitive), and — most interestingly — proving that your permutation / paraphrase rules are sound: that any reordering licensed by the case system provably preserves the structure. That's a small, beautiful, self-contained theorem. C works if you keep it this narrow; it fails if you reach for “meaning.”

7.The sharp warm-up you didn't ask about — Piṅgala's meter Best first project

Before any of A/B/C, there's a target that is decidable, mathematically gorgeous, culturally deep, and — crucially — already half-proved in Lean's standard library. It's Sanskrit prosody (chandas), systematized by Piṅgala (c. 300–200 BCE; the dating and how much is later commentary by Halāyudha is genuinely debated, so hedge it).

Piṅgala classified each syllable as laghu (light, 1 mora, L) or guru (heavy, 2 morae, G) by a purely local rule, and enumerated meters as binary L/G strings. This is the earliest known binary representation — and it hides three jewels:

Why this is the perfect first Lean project:

  • It's decidable and small. Define an inductive type of L/G strings; define M n by the mora recurrence; the meter-matching predicate is a simple structural check.
  • The deep theorem is already in Mathlib. The mātrāmeru ↔ Fibonacci bridge is literally Nat.fib_succ_eq_sum_choose; the 2ⁿ enumeration is Nat.sum_range_choose; the recurrence is Nat.fib_add_two. You mostly model, then connect.
  • You prove a real, named, historical theorem — “the number of n-mora meters is the Virahāṅka–Hemachandra (Fibonacci) number” — with a machine-checked proof. That's a publishable, tweetable artifact and a fully self-contained warm-up that teaches you the toolchain.
-- the shape of the Piṅgala warm-up (Lean 4 / Mathlib), schematically
inductive Syl | laghu | guru          -- light = 1 mora, heavy = 2 morae

def morae : List Syl → Nat
  | []            => 0
  | .laghu :: xs  => 1 + morae xs
  | .guru  :: xs  => 2 + morae xs

-- M n := number of L/G strings whose total duration is exactly n morae

8.A concrete ladder to climb

Put together, the honest plan is a difficulty ladder, each rung teaching what the next needs, and every rung chosen so that Lean is doing something a Python script can't:

Are any of these stupid? No — but two need discipline.
  • A is genuinely good and genuinely novel (no prior proof-assistant Pāṇini), provided you target meta-theorems about the rule system, not “properties of a sentence.”
  • B is real but seductive: the obvious version doesn't need Lean. Only the proof-carrying version does — and it depends on A.
  • C is real but only if “same” = same kāraka structure. Reach for “meaning” and it dissolves into undecidability.
  • The biggest risk across all three is rebuilding what Huet/Kulkarni already did. Parsing, splitting, and scanning are solved. Stay strictly on the side of proof, where the field is empty.
