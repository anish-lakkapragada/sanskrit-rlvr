Going Deeper: What's Actually
Provable About Pāṇini?

Part II — the claimed properties of Direction A and whether anyone has ever proved them; how Direction A really feeds Direction B; and whether kāraka equivalence rides along for free.

A working note · Part II · 2026

This is the companion to the first note. You asked the harder questions: are termination, confluence, uniqueness, and asiddhatva-faithfulness genuinely claimed properties — by whom, and with what proof? How exactly does a verified grammar (A) turn into a reward signal (B)? What is a “sūtra derivation,” concretely? And can kāraka equivalence just be a small rider on A? I went and read the literature. One answer forces me to walk back a word from Part I.

Correction up front — “confluence” was the wrong claim. Part I listed confluence (Church–Rosser) — “order of rule application doesn't matter” — as a target for Direction A. The scholarship is decisive that this is backwards: Pāṇini's grammar is deliberately non-confluent. Order does matter, and the grammar carries an explicit apparatus (vipratiṣedha 1.4.2, asiddhatva/the tripādī 8.2.1, and the paribhāṣā strength hierarchy) whose entire job is to break ties and force a unique result anyway. The right property to prove is therefore determinism-under-control, not confluence. Section 2 fixes this properly — it's a more interesting target than the wrong one was.

1.The honest scorecard

Here is the result of the literature sweep in one table. The column that matters is the last one. The headline: almost nothing about the grammar's derivational behaviour has ever been proved — most “properties” are asserted by tradition or argued informally, and not a single part of Pāṇini has been mechanized in any theorem prover (Lean, Coq, Isabelle, Agda). That is the vacuum your project would enter.

Read the table as an opportunity map, not a graveyard. “Asserted but never proven” is exactly the raw material a formalization project converts into results — and the one cell marked PROVEN-but-not-mechanized (Petersen) is the lowest-risk first theorem you could pick. More on that in §6.

2.Direction A, corrected — the four properties

2.1  Why it's controlled determinism, not confluence

A confluent rewriting system has the diamond property: wherever two rules both apply, the two resulting paths re-converge, so the final normal form is independent of your choices. Pāṇini's grammar is engineered for the opposite situation — two rules clash, the two paths diverge, and a meta-rule legislates a winner. The very existence of that legislation is the proof that the system is not confluent.

The control machinery, all of which you'd model in Lean:

  • vipratiṣedha — sūtra 1.4.2, “vipratiṣedhe paraṃ kāryam”: Pāṇini's only explicitly stated tie-break meta-rule. On the traditional reading, when two rules of equal strength conflict, the one later in the text wins. (Rajpopat's 2022 Cambridge thesis re-reads “para” as “the operation on the right-hand operand,” but either way it's a deterministic tie-breaker — the point is that one is needed at all.)
  • asiddhatva + the tripādī — sūtra 8.2.1, “pūrvatrāsiddham”: the last three quarter-books (8.2–8.4) are declared “as if not having applied” to what precedes — which fixes a mandatory application order in the tail of the grammar.
  • the paribhāṣā strength hierarchy (mostly codified by the commentators): when one rule is nitya (obligatory), antaraṅga (inner-conditioned), or apavāda (a specific exception), it beats its anitya / bahiraṅga / utsarga counterpart — so 1.4.2 never even fires. Kiparsky formalizes the exception case precisely as domain-inclusion blocking.
The mixed-architecture nuance worth getting right. The grammar is not uniformly ordered. The siddhakāṇḍa (the main body, 1.1.1–8.1.74) holds rules mutually siddha — textual order there largely does not drive derivation, a confluence-like regime. Only the tripādī tail is strictly ordered by asiddhatva. So an accurate Lean statement is: “the sandhi subsystem is deterministic — given the conflict-resolution lattice, every input has a unique normal form — even though it is not freely confluent.” That is a sharper and more honest theorem than Part I's.

2.2  The four properties, with sources and proof status

PropertyWhat it would say in LeanSource & status

The transferable machinery is real: general & context-free grammars are formalized in Lean 4; confluence/termination of rewriting is mature (CoLoR in Coq; recent Lean 4 confluence frameworks). Pointing it at Pāṇini is the novelty.

3.What a “sūtra derivation” actually is

This term does a lot of work in Direction B, so here it is concretely. A prakriyā (derivation) is the step-by-step construction of a word, starting from a verbal root (dhātu) or nominal stem (prātipadika) plus affixes, applying Aṣṭādhyāyī sūtras one at a time until the surface form appears. Each step is a pair: (the string so far, the sūtra that licenses the next change).

One subtlety you'll meet: Pāṇini orders his sūtras for economy (the Aṣṭādhyāyī-krama), not in the order a derivation needs them. The later prakriyā tradition — above all Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita's Siddhānta-Kaumudī — re-sequences the same sūtras into derivation order (the prakriyā-krama). Same rules, teaching-friendly order.

Crucially, this is not hypothetical — engines already generate exactly these sequences: the Vidyut/Ambuda prakriya library, Kulkarni's Saṃsādhanī Prakriyāpradarśinī, the Goyal–Kulkarni Aṣṭādhyāyī Simulator, and a recent fast prakriyā generator (ISCLS 2024). A Lean checker would verify the same datatype: a list of (string, sūtra-id) steps, replaying each cited sūtra to confirm it produces the next string.

4.Direction A ⇒ Direction B — the link, spelled out

You said you're still unclear how A implies B. Here it is in one sentence: the verified artifact you build in A is precisely the reward function B needs — and because it's verified, it's a reward that can't be gamed. Let me unpack the “why Lean, not Python” because that's the crux.

4.1  The honest tension

You're right that the reward metric doesn't require Lean. A prakriyā checker can be a Python function: replay each cited sūtra, return 1 if the steps reach the claimed form, else 0. The prakriya library essentially is that. So for vanilla RLVR, plain code suffices. If that's all you want, don't use Lean.

4.2  The three things Lean adds that Python can't

PayoffWhy it needs the prover
An un-game-able grader RL agents are ruthless at exploiting bugs in the reward. A Python checker with an edge-case error becomes a reward-hack target. In A you prove the checker correct (sound & complete against your modeled grammar), so there is no grader bug to exploit. The reward is trustworthy by theorem.
Dense, step-level reward A derivation is a chain of checkable steps, not one opaque answer. Lean's relation s₁ —[sūtra]→ s₂ is decidable per step, so you reward partial derivations and localize the first wrong sūtra — the “process-verified” signal recent work shows beats sparse 0/1 (the AXLE / process-reward line).
A verified data flywheel Every derivation the model produces that type-checks is, by construction, correct training data — a growing corpus of machine-verified prakriyās with no label noise and no model-collapse risk. This is the AXLE thesis applied to grammar instead of math.
So the implication is: A produces (i) a formal grammar, (ii) a checker that's proven correct against it, and (iii) a notion of “valid derivation” as a checkable proof object. B is just “run RLVR with (ii) as the reward and (iii) as the output format.” The dependency is real and one-directional: B's soundness is literally A's theorem. Skip A, and B degrades to “RL against a Python script you didn't verify” — which works, but is no longer a Lean project and inherits every bug in the script as a reward-hack.

5.Direction C — does kāraka equivalence come free with A?

Your question: can we just prove kāraka is an equivalence class as a small part of A, and can it be done consistently? The honest answer has a pleasant half and a subtle half.

5.1  The pleasant half: the equivalence axioms are nearly free

Define two sentences as equivalent when they share the same kāraka structure (the who-did-what-to-whom — the śābdabodha). Formally, that's A ~ B ⇔ kāraka(A) = kāraka(B) — equivalence defined as equality of a derived object. In Lean, reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity then fall out automatically, because they're inherited from Eq (this is the standard Setoid / kernel-of-a-function pattern). So “prove kāraka is an equivalence relation” is, by itself, almost a one-liner. The mathematical content is not in the three axioms.

-- equivalence-by-a-function: the three axioms are inherited, not earned
def sameKaraka (a b : Sentence) : Prop := karaka a = karaka b

instance : Equivalence sameKaraka :=
  InvImage.equivalence karaka (by exact eq_equivalence)   -- refl/symm/trans, free

5.2  The subtle half: where the real work (and the consistency risk) lives

The content — and the danger — is entirely in defining karaka well. Here the literature flags a hard fact: the vibhakti (case-ending) ↔ kāraka mapping is many-to-many, in both directions.

DirectionExampleConsequence
one case → many roles the instrumental marks karaṇa (“by horse”, aśvena) but kartṛ in the passive (nareṇa “by the man”) you cannot read roles off the surface cases
one role → many cases kartṛ surfaces as nominative (active) or instrumental (passive) depending on prayoga (voice) active & passive sentences must collapse to the same structure — that's the goal — but the map isn't a function on the surface

So karaka is not a well-defined function on raw strings — which is exactly why defining equivalence at the surface case level would be inconsistent. The fix is the same move that makes A work: quotient at the right level.

5.3  Verdict — yes, and it's genuinely a small rider on A

Can it be a small part of A? Yes — it reuses A's exact proof pattern. A is already in the business of “define a normal form; prove rewrite rules preserve it.” Kāraka equivalence is the same shape: define the canonical structure (the normal form), prove the paraphrase rules (active↔passive, permutation) sound. The equivalence-relation property is free; consistency is bought by quotienting at the disambiguated kāraka level, not the vibhakti level. The one caveat: building a total, correct disambiguator is itself hard (it's Kulkarni's constraint-parser problem) — so scope C to a fragment where disambiguation is unambiguous (e.g. fully case-marked clauses, active↔passive, reordering), and it's a clean, self-contained add-on. Reach for full natural-language paraphrase and it explodes.

6.The single most fundable first theorem

If you want one concrete target where the risk is lowest and the “first ever” claim is cleanest, it is not termination or faithfulness (asserted, hard, contested). It is mechanizing Petersen's Śivasūtra optimality theorem in Lean:

  • The proof already exists (Petersen 2004), so you're not gambling on whether the result is true — you're porting a known pen-and-paper proof to Lean. That's a tractable, bounded project.
  • It's a real theorem about Pāṇini, not a toy: Pāṇini's ordering of the phonemes is the minimal arrangement letting every natural class be named by a contiguous pratyāhāra. Lattice / Formal Concept Analysis — and Mathlib has the order-theory.
  • It would be the first mechanized result about Pāṇini in any prover — an unambiguous, defensible contribution, and a perfect on-ramp to the harder determinism theorems for sandhi (the real Direction A flagship).

Then the ladder is: Śivasūtra optimality (port a known proof) → determinism of a sandhi fragment under 1.4.2 + asiddhatva (the genuinely new Direction-A theorem) → kāraka equivalence (the §5 rider) → proof-carrying RLVR (Direction B). Each rung's verified artifact is the next rung's foundation, and every rung is doing something a Python script provably cannot.
