Pāṇini in Lean 4:
Five Machine-Checked Results, Rated by Controversy

The first mechanization of any part of Pāṇini's Sanskrit grammar in a theorem prover — from a settled Fibonacci fact to a kernel-checked refutation that brushes against the published literature.

1.What this repository is

Around the 4th century BCE, Pāṇini compressed a complete grammar of Sanskrit into ~4,000 rules — the Aṣṭādhyāyī. Its front matter, the 14 Śivasūtras, arranges every sound of Sanskrit so that any sound-class his rules need can be named by just two letters (a pratyāhāra). This repository formalizes that system in Lean 4 — the 42 sounds, the 14 marker letters, the strict pratyāhāra semantics, and the 43 sound-classes the grammar actually uses — and proves five families of theorems about it. Everything is sorry-free and rests only on the standard axioms propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound; the heavy combinatorial claims are replayed by the Lean kernel itself (decide), not by tactics you have to trust.

Each result lives on its own showcase branch with a full README and an interactive explainer. This page is the map: what each branch proves, and an honest controversy rating — because these theorems do not all have the same standing. Some confirm what everyone already believed; one of them machine-checks a counterexample to the natural reading of a published claim.

2.The controversy scale

RatingMeaning
1 — SettledThe mathematics and the history are undisputed; the formalization adds certainty, not controversy.
2 — MildConfirms the consensus, but corrects a detail in the published literature along the way.
3 — InterpretiveFormalizes one side of an ancient debate; the verdict depends on a modeling choice a scholar could reasonably reject.
4 — Live disputeTakes a quantitative position in an active scholarly controversy; both camps have something to contest.
5 — Against the literatureContains a machine-checked result that contradicts the natural reading of a published claim or received doctrine.

4.The results, in order of rising controversy

The Virahāṅka–Hemacandra (Fibonacci) theorem

branch: result/pingala-fibonacci · file: panini/Panini/Pingala.lean

1 / 5 — settled

In Sanskrit prosody a light syllable (laghu) lasts one beat and a heavy syllable (guru) two. Theorem: the light/heavy patterns of total duration n number exactly Nat.fib (n+1). Mechanized as the genuine combinatorial statement, not just the recurrence: Virahāṅka's enumeration is proved sound, complete, and duplicate-free (mem_patterns, nodup_patterns, length_patterns), alongside Piṅgala's prastāra count of 2^n syllable patterns. The recursion was stated by Virahāṅka (c. 700 CE) and elaborated by Gopāla and Hemacandra (c. 1150) — Fibonacci's Liber Abaci is from 1202.

What a skeptic would say: almost nothing. The mathematics is textbook and the historical priority is documented in the standard history-of-mathematics literature. The only care needed is attribution — Piṅgala's own text (3rd–2nd c. BCE) gives the enumeration cryptically, and the explicit recursion is Virahāṅka's, which is exactly how this branch names things.
Sources
  • Parmanand Singh, “The So-called Fibonacci Numbers in Ancient and Medieval India”, Historia Mathematica 12 (1985) 229–244 — open PDF.

No anubandha is redundant (marker irredundancy)

branch: result/marker-irredundancy · file: panini/Panini/Necessity.lean

2 / 5 — mild

The tradition's economy principle (lāghava — “grammarians rejoice over the saving of half a mora as over the birth of a son”) asserts nothing in Pāṇini is superfluous. At the Śivasūtra level this is now a theorem: no_marker_redundant shows that deleting any one of the 14 markers breaks a class the grammar actually uses — for each truncated alphabet the kernel searches all 588 candidate encodings and confirms none rescues the witness class. noH2_not_lax/strict shows the duplicated h can't be dropped either, under any reading. And the kernel counts the Śivasūtras' well-formed pratyāhāras exactly: 304, of which 13 are singletons.

What a skeptic would say: the headline confirms what everyone from Kātyāyana onward believed, so no fight there. The mild friction is the count: Petersen (2004, fn. 2) reports 305 / 14, counting by marker occurrences; this formalization counts by denotation-relevant type and gets 304 / 13. It's a definitional correction to a published number, not a refutation — but it is a discrepancy with the literature that the kernel now adjudicates.
Sources
  • Wiebke Petersen, “A Mathematical Analysis of Pāṇini's Śivasūtras”, J. of Logic, Language and Information 13 (2004) 471–489, fn. 2 — open PDF.
  • On lāghava: George Cardona, “Studies in Indian Grammarians I: The Method of Description Reflected in the Śivasūtras”, Trans. Am. Philos. Soc. 59.1 (1969) 3–48.

The doubled Ṇ is irreducibly ambiguous

branch: result/doubled-n-ambiguity · file: panini/Panini/Ambiguity.lean

3 / 5 — interpretive

As actually recited, lines 1 and 6 of the Śivasūtras end in the same marker letter Ṇ — the only re-use, and one Kātyāyana and Patañjali already questioned (Mahābhāṣya, c. 150 BCE: was there a “dearth of letters”?). Modeling the recitation faithfully (one Ṇ token), the kernel shows exactly 3 pratyāhāras are ambiguous (aṆ, iṆ, uṆ); the uniform nearest-Ṇ convention makes an attested class unencodable, the uniform farthest-Ṇ convention kills another, Pāṇini's own rules need both readings (1.1.69 vs. 8.3.57), and only rule-by-rule resolution serves all 43 attested classes. That is Patañjali's complaint as a theorem: the economy of one saved letter is purchased with context-dependence in the metalanguage.

What a skeptic would say: the verdict hangs on a modeling choice. Most formal treatments (including Petersen's, and the other branches here) simply treat the two Ṇ's as distinct symbols, dissolving the ambiguity by fiat — a Pāṇinian can argue that markers are individuated by position, not letter-shape, so the “irreducibility” is an artifact of the single-token model. The reply is that the single-token model is what is recited, and the ancients themselves debated it — but that is a genuine interpretive stance, not a neutral fact.
Sources
  • The Mahābhāṣya discussion of the Śivasūtras (Kātyāyana's vārttikas and Patañjali's commentary on the re-used Ṇ), surveyed in: George Cardona, “Studies in Indian Grammarians I”, TAPS 59.1 (1969).
  • Paul Kiparsky, “Economy and the Construction of the Śivasūtras” (1991), on the anubandha system and its economy.
  • Rules using both readings: Aṣṭādhyāyī 1.1.69 (far reading of aṆ) vs. its use elsewhere (near reading), 8.3.57 (iṆ, far reading).

The forced/free map of the Śivasūtra ordering

branch: result/ordering-forced-free · file: panini/Panini/Ordering.lean

4 / 5 — live dispute

How much of Pāṇini's ordering is actually determined by the class system? Kiparsky argues economy makes the order essentially unique; Staal and Cardona hold that independent phonetic principles must be doing real work. This branch settles the checkable core with a kernel-verified map of all 29 adjacent within-line transpositions: 11 are FORCED (each by a witness class plus an exhaustive 588-encoding kernel search — including the h-y, y-v, v-r verdicts that pin Patañjali's ancient ha ya va ra question) and 18 are FREE (each by explicit surviving pratyāhāras for all 43 classes). Verdict: economy pins the within-line order at 11 of 29 junctures and leaves 18 — including such “obvious” pairs as e-o and ai-au — to phonetics or tradition.

What a skeptic would say: both camps can object, which is what makes this the most actively contestable branch. A Kiparskyan will note that “economy” here means only survival of the 43 attested classes under adjacent within-line swaps — Kiparsky's uniqueness argument also invokes marker economy and global rearrangements, which this map deliberately does not measure. A Staal/Cardona partisan will counter that 18 free junctures still under-counts the phonetic contribution, since line membership itself was held fixed. The branch's own framing — a “quantitative middle verdict” — is precisely the kind of claim both sides of a live dispute have reason to push back on.
Sources
  • Paul Kiparsky, “Economy and the Construction of the Śivasūtras”, in Pāṇinian Studies: Prof. S. D. Joshi Felicitation Volume (Ann Arbor, 1991) — the uniqueness side.
  • J. F. Staal, “A Method of Linguistic Description: The Order of Consonants According to Pāṇini”, Language 38.1 (1962) 1–10 — the phonetic-principles side.
  • George Cardona, “Studies in Indian Grammarians I”, TAPS 59.1 (1969) 3–48.
  • Wiebke Petersen, “A Mathematical Analysis of Pāṇini's Śivasūtras”, JoLLI 13 (2004), on what the class system does and does not determine.

The Śivasūtra optimality theorem — and a machine-checked refutation

branch: result/shivasutra-optimality · files: panini/Panini/{Interval,Markers,Optimality}.lean

5 / 5 — against the literature

Petersen (2004/2008) proved the Śivasūtras optimal: fewest duplicated sounds first, fewest markers second. This branch mechanizes the theorem's skeleton unconditionally: duplication is forced for any rival (one_le_duplications_for_panini, via a convexity obstruction on h, v, l), so Pāṇini's single repeated h is exactly optimal (shivasutra_duplication_optimal, hypothesis-free); every strict rival carries ≥ 11 markers and costs ≥ 12 against Pāṇini's 15. But it also contains the repository's most explosive artifact:

lax_marker_bound_false: under the naive, marker-optional reading of “encodes all the pratyāhāras” — the natural formalization of the claim Petersen's 2004 paper calls obvious — deleting the final marker L yields a valid 13-marker rival that beats Pāṇini by 2. Kernel-checked. Under that semantics, the Śivasūtras are not optimal, full stop.

The branch's response is that the lax semantics is the wrong arena, and it develops the strict semantics (EncodesT) throughout. Full cost-optimality is then proved conditional on the tight bound 14 ≤ numMarkers — Petersen's Satz 6.1.1, whose proof (plane Hasse diagrams, S-graphs, boundary runs) remains unmechanized and is carefully diagnosed, with two honest caveats, at the end of Optimality.lean.

What a skeptic would say: three things, each sharper than anything on the other branches. (i) The refutation touches a published claim — the 2004 JoLLI paper treats the 14-marker count as “obvious,” and only the 2008 dissertation proves it, under assumptions. (ii) The rescue is contestable: the strict semantics could be accused of being chosen because it makes Pāṇini optimal. (iii) The headline theorem is conditional: Satz 6.1.1 is proved by Petersen only relative to duplication-minimal extensions, and in a model whose pratyāhāras may start at a chosen copy of a sound rather than the first — so her bound does not transfer verbatim, and the tight strict bound remains genuinely open here. It also brushes the tradition itself: the necessity of the final L is a centuries-old debate among the grammarians, and this is the result that formally feeds it.
Sources
  • Wiebke Petersen, “A Mathematical Analysis of Pāṇini's Śivasūtras”, J. of Logic, Language and Information 13 (2004) 471–489 — open PDF.
  • Wiebke Petersen, Zur Minimalität von Pāṇinis Śivasūtras — Eine Untersuchung mit Methoden der Formalen Begriffsanalyse, dissertation, Univ. Düsseldorf 2008 (Satz 6.1.1: pp. 149–152) — open PDF (DNB).
  • Paul Kiparsky, “Economy and the Construction of the Śivasūtras” (1991), on the economy criteria the optimality claim formalizes.

5.How the ratings were assigned

The rating measures how much a competent scholar could dispute, not how hard the theorem was to prove. Three questions drive it:

  • Does it contradict anything? Confirming consensus (Fibonacci, irredundancy) scores low; correcting a published count scores a little higher; a kernel-checked counterexample to the natural reading of a published optimality claim scores highest.
  • Is the dispute alive? Patañjali's Ṇ question is ancient and the tradition already settled on rule-by-rule resolution (3); Kiparsky vs. Staal/Cardona is a live modern disagreement that this repo takes a quantitative position in (4).
  • How load-bearing are the modeling choices? Where a differently-reasonable formalization flips the verdict (single-token Ṇ; lax vs. strict semantics), the rating rises — the kernel guarantees the theorem, never the choice of statement.
One caution the ratings share: a Lean kernel check makes the theorem indisputable, given the model. Every controversy above lives in the gap between Pāṇini's Sanskrit and the Lean model — the 43-class list, the strict semantics, the single-token recitation. That gap is documented, per branch, in each branch's README and in the source files' closing comments.

6.Building and verifying

git checkout <branch>     # any of the five result/* branches, or main
cd panini
lake exe cache get         # download prebuilt Mathlib
lake build                 # replays every proof through the Lean kernel

All results are sorry-free; the axiom footprint of every theorem is propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound (checked with #print axioms). Each showcase branch's own README.html is an interactive explainer — the optimality branch has a clickable pratyāhāra explorer, the irredundancy branch lets you delete markers yourself, the ordering branch lets you perform the swaps, and the ambiguity branch lets you read the doubled Ṇ both ways.
