The Architecture of Sanskritसंस्कृतम्

a language assembled, sound by sound, like a machine
❖ ❖ ❖
विद्या ददाति विनयम्
vidyā dadāti vinayam
“Knowledge bestows humility.” — a सुभाषित (well-spoken verse)

Sanskrit — saṃskṛta, “perfected, refined” — is not merely an old language. Around the fifth century BCE the grammarian Pāṇini set it down as some four thousand ordered rules that generate the language: where each sound may stand, how words bend to their role, how neighbouring sounds melt into one another. It is, in effect, the first formal grammar ever written — a poem and a program at once. What follows is a short walk through its architecture, not a catalogue: the six moving parts that make a Sanskrit sentence click into place.

Read this in two voices. Alongside each idea you will find the actual Lean 4 from the companion MiniSanskrit formalization — every snippet labelled with its source file. The grammar on the left of your mind; its machine-checked encoding on the right. Where the prose says “sounds fuse by exact rules,” the Lean shows the very function that fuses them.

To these are added the four semivowels य र ल व (ya ra la va), three sibilants श ष स (śa ṣa sa), and the aspirate ह (ha). This grid is not decoration: nearly every rule that follows is phrased as “same place of articulation” or “the voiced counterpart.”

In Lean, that whole inventory is one inductive type — each sound a constructor, grouped by the very same vargas. Modelling sounds as constructors (not characters) is deliberate: “kh” and “ṛ” are single phonemes.

inductive Phoneme where
  -- vowels (svara)
  | a | aa | i | ii | u | uu | rv | rrv | lv | e | ai | o | au
  -- velar · palatal · retroflex · dental · labial (the five vargas)
  | k | kh | g | gh | velarN
  | c | ch | j | jh | palatalN
  | retroT | retroTh | retroD | retroDh | retroN
  | t | th | d | dh | n
  | p | ph | b | bh | m
  -- semivowels · sibilants · aspirate · anusvāra ṃ · visarga ḥ
  | y | r | l | v | palatalS | retroS | s | h | anusvara | visarga
deriving DecidableEq, Repr, BEq

-- the grid pays off: voicing is a derived predicate, used all over sandhi
def voicedCons : Phoneme → Bool
  | g | gh | velarN | j | jh | palatalN | retroD | retroDh | retroN
  | d | dh | n | b | bh | m | y | r | l | v | h => true
  | _ => false
Part Two

The Ladder of Vowelsगुण · वृद्धि

Sanskrit builds words by strengthening vowels through three grades. This quiet little ladder — basic, guṇa, vṛddhi — is the engine behind half the grammar: it turns roots into nouns, presents into perfects, simple forms into causatives.

basic
इ / उ / ऋ
i · u · ṛ
guṇa — strengthened
ए / ओ / अर्
e · o · ar
vṛddhi — lengthened
ऐ / औ / आर्
ai · au · ār

So the root भू bhū “to be,” strengthened to guṇa, yields the present stem भव् bhav- → भवति bhavati “he is.” The same ladder, climbed once more, gives the grand vṛddhi derivatives of classical poetry.

You can see the ladder itself living inside the vowel-sandhi function: when an a meets an i, the result is the guṇa grade e; meeting e, the vṛddhi grade ai.

MiniSanskrit/Sandhi.lean — vowelSandhi
  -- guṇa after a/ā
  else if (lhs == a || lhs == aa) && (rhs == i || rhs == ii) then [e]   -- a + i → e
  else if (lhs == a || lhs == aa) && (rhs == u || rhs == uu) then [o]   -- a + u → o
  -- vṛddhi after a/ā
  else if (lhs == a || lhs == aa) && (rhs == e || rhs == ai) then [ai]  -- a + e → ai
  else if (lhs == a || lhs == aa) && (rhs == o || rhs == au) then [au]  -- a + o → au
Part Three

The Melting of Soundsसन्धि

Here is the part that makes Sanskrit feel as though it compiles. Words are not left to sit side by side: where one ends and the next begins, the sounds fuse by exact, predictable rules called sandhi (“joining”). You write the words in their plain form; sandhi renders what is actually spoken.

न+अस्ति →नास्ति savarṇa-dīrgha
na + asti → nāsti · “is not”
इति+उक्तम् →इत्युक्तम् yaṇ: i → y
iti + uktam → ityuktam · “thus said”
रामः+गच्छति →रामो गच्छति visarga: aḥ → o
rāmaḥ + gacchati → rāmo gacchati
रामः+च →रामश्च visarga → palatal ś
rāmaḥ + ca → rāmaś ca · “and Rāma”
Two words meet, and a third sound is born — deterministically. That determinism is exactly why a machine can check whether a sentence is “spelled” correctly.

That determinism is total functions. Visarga sandhi — the rāmaḥ + gacchati → rāmo gacchati rule above — is a handful of branches; and sandhi2 fuses any two words by trying the rules in fixed Pāṇinian order.

MiniSanskrit/Sandhi.lean
-- vow is the vowel before the visarga ḥ; q :: rest is what follows
def visargaResolve (vow q : Phoneme) (rest : Word) : Word :=
  if vow == a then
    if q == a        then o :: rest          -- aḥ + a → o
    else if q.isVowel  then o :: q :: rest     -- aḥ + V → o V
    else if q.voiced   then o :: q :: rest     -- aḥ + voiced → o  (rāmo gacchati)
    else visargaPlace a q rest
  ...

-- fuse two adjacent words: rules tried in a fixed (ordered) priority
def sandhi2 (l rt : Word) : Word :=
  match l.reverse, rt with
  | last :: revInit, q :: rest =>
    if last == visarga    then ...                        -- visarga sandhi
    else if last.isVowel  then ... vowelSandhi last q ...  -- vowel sandhi
    else if last == m      then ... anusvara :: q :: rest    -- m + cons → ṃ
    else if last == t      then ... tSandhi q ...            -- t-sandhi
    else l ++ rt
  | _, _ => ...
Part Four

The Eightfold Caseविभक्ति

A Sanskrit noun does not lean on word order to show its job in the sentence. It wears its role as an ending. There are eight such vibhakti, illustrated here on देव deva, “god.”

The singular of deva — one stem, eight roles.
№CaseFormSense
1nominativeदेवः devaḥthe god (does)
2accusativeदेवम् devamthe god (is acted on)
3instrumentalदेवेन devenaby / with the god
4dativeदेवाय devāyafor / to the god
5ablativeदेवात् devātfrom the god
6genitiveदेवस्य devasyaof the god
7locativeदेवे devein / on the god
8vocativeहे देव he devaO god!

Behind the cases lies a beautiful idea — Pāṇini’s kāraka theory: every verb opens a small set of semantic roles (the doer, the done-to, the instrument, the recipient, the source, the location), and each role is dressed in its proper case. Grammar becomes a mapping from meaning to ending.

Each declension is one total lookup table — a stem’s base plus the ending for every (case, number). The comments are the IAST of each cell, so the table can be proof-read by eye against the one above.

MiniSanskrit/Morphology.lean
structure NounEntry where
  stem   : Word        -- deva = [d, e, v, a]
  gender : Gender
  decl   : Decl

-- a-stem masculine (deva "god")
def declineAMasc (b : Word) : Case → Number → Word
  | .nom, .sg => b ++ [a, visarga]          -- devaḥ
  | .acc, .sg => b ++ [a, m]                -- devam
  | .ins, .sg => b ++ [e, n, a]             -- devena
  | .dat, .sg => b ++ [aa, y, a]            -- devāya
  ...
  | .loc, .pl => b ++ [e, retroS, u]        -- deveṣu

-- one dispatcher over all six declension classes
def decline (e : NounEntry) (cas : Case) (nm : Number) : Word :=
  let b := base e.stem
  match e.decl with
  | .aMasc => declineAMasc b cas nm
  | .iiFem => declineIiFem b cas nm
  | ...
Part Five

The Living Verbक्रिया

The verb agrees with its doer in person and number, and shifts its whole shape to speak of fact, command, wish, or time. From the single present stem गच्छ gaccha- (“go”) the language draws a whole spectrum of mood:

Mood / tenseFormMeaning
presentगच्छति gacchatihe goes
imperfect (past)अगच्छत् agacchathe went
futureगमिष्यति gamiṣyatihe will go
imperativeगच्छ gacchago!
optative (wish)गच्छेत् gacchethe should go

And it conjugates across three persons and — uniquely — three numbers, for Sanskrit keeps a true dual beside the singular and plural: भवति bhavati (one is), भवतः bhavataḥ (two are), भवन्ति bhavanti (many are).

One conjugate function draws all five moods from the stem: the imperative and optative add their own endings, the imperfect prepends the augment a-, and the future swaps in its own stem.

MiniSanskrit/Verbs.lean
-- present-tense active endings (the doer's person × number)
def presParaEnd : Person → Number → Word
  | .third, .sg => [a, t, i]     -- gacchati  "he goes"
  | .third, .pl => [a, n, t, i]  -- gacchanti "they go"
  | .first, .sg => [aa, m, i]    -- gacchāmi  "I go"
  | ...

def conjugate (ve : VerbEntry) (ten : Tense) (per : Person) (nm : Number) : Word :=
  let pres := base ve.presentStem
  match ten, ve.pada with
  | .pres, .para => pres ++ presParaEnd per nm           -- gacchati
  | .impv, _     => pres ++ impvParaEnd per nm           -- gaccha  "go!"
  | .opt,  _     => pres ++ optParaEnd  per nm           -- gacchet "should go"
  | .impf, _     => a :: (pres ++ impfParaEnd per nm)    -- agacchat (augment a-)
  | .fut,  _     => base ve.futureStem ++ presParaEnd per nm  -- gamiṣyati
  | .pres, .atma => pres ++ presAtmaEnd per nm           -- labhate (middle voice)
Part Six

Freedom of Orderपदक्रम

Because the endings carry the roles, the words may dance. Here is a complete sentence, its three roles painted in three colours:

a complete sentence
रामः फलं खादति
rāmaḥ phalaṃ khādati
“Rāma eats the fruit.”
agent · nominative object · accusative verb · agrees with the agent

Reverse the nouns — फलं रामः खादति phalaṃ rāmaḥ khādati — and the meaning does not budge. The fruit is still eaten, because रामः is marked as the agent and फलं as the object, wherever they sit. Order becomes a matter of emphasis and music, not of grammar — which is why Sanskrit verse can arrange its words for beauty without ever losing its sense.

This is why the checker reasons over features, never position. A sentence is a list of tokens (each a word plus its features); isGrammatical asks that the verb agree with its nominative agent and that the cases be licensed — order irrelevant. And because it is a decidable boolean, a sentence’s correctness can be proved:

MiniSanskrit/Syntax.lean & Checker.lean
inductive Token
  | noun (en : NounEntry) (cas : Case) (nm : Number)
  | verb (ve : VerbEntry) (ten : Tense) (per : Person) (nm : Number)
  | ...                          -- pronouns, adjectives, particles

def isGrammatical (ts : Sentence) : Bool :=
  let subs := inCase .nom (heads ts)   -- the nominative agent(s)
  ...
  (match subs, vinfos ts with
   | sh :: _, [vb] => (vb.per == sh.per) && (vb.num == sh.num)  -- agreement, not order
   | _, _ => true) && ...

…so “Rāma eats the fruit” is not asserted — it is proved, by computation, in the kernel:

MiniSanskrit/Examples.lean
def s1 : Sentence := [noun raama .nom .sg, noun phala .acc .sg, verb khaad .pres .third .sg]

-- रामः फलं खादति  —  machine-checked to be grammatical
theorem s1_ok : isGrammatical s1 = true := by decide
❧ ❧ ❧

Six moving parts — वर्ण sounds, the vowel ladder, सन्धि euphony, विभक्ति case, the living verb, and free order — interlock into a language precise enough to be computed. A sentence is “correct” when its sounds combine lawfully, its words wear the right endings, and verb and agent agree.

śubham astu — may it be auspicious.
